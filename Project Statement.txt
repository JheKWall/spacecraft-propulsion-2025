The goal of this study is to determine which mission role each category of spacecraft propulsion
is best suited for, comparing chemical, electric, and nuclear systems on specific impulse, thrust,
input power, and thrust-to-weight ratio, and distinguishing proven hardware from prototype and
theoretical concepts, using published figures current as of 2025.


ORIGINAL STATEMENT (retired)

The goal of this study is to determine the most cost-effective spacecraft propulsion system based
on 2025 statistics.

Retired for two reasons, both established during data collection.

No per-system cost data exists. Across every source consulted, the only dollar figures are
programme-level history ($7 billion for Project Rover through 1972; $11 million for ORION) and a
generic $10,000/kg launch cost. There is no cost figure attributable to any individual engine, and
none exists publicly for the theoretical concepts, several of which have never been built.

The phrase "2025 statistics" was also unsupportable. The original project's figures came from a
2003 paper, which remains the only source publishing comparable numbers for the theoretical
systems.

The replacement question is one the data can actually answer, and it is a stronger result than a
ranking: nothing is best in the abstract, only best for a job.

See docs/data-collection.md for the full reasoning.
